# Validation Signals by Product Type

Load this in Mode A Phase 2 and in Mode B when drafting or checking Key Results. Pick the
section that matches the founder's product type (company ground truth, `02_Product`, field
"Product type"). For a hybrid (a smart water bottle is hardware plus an app), use the section
where the riskiest assumption lives and borrow one signal from the other.

Ground rules for every type:

- A signal must come from a real person doing or saying something specific, not from the
  founder's own opinion or from friends being polite.
- **Prototypes take no real payments.** Use stated intent, waitlists, letters of intent (LOIs),
  and price-ladder answers. Do not charge cards or take pre-orders as money. Real retail or
  online sales come later, after the compliance and legal checks for your category.
- Keep the sample honest: "5 of 8 strangers" beats "everyone loved it". Say how many people.
- The counter-example in each row is what founders often count that does not prove anything.

## Software (apps, platforms, tools)

Typical riskiest assumption: people will change a habit to use it.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task completion | Moderated click-through of the prototype; time and drop-off points | 6 of 8 target users complete the core task unaided in under 5 minutes | "Users said the screens look clean" |
| Activation | Watch whether a new user reaches their first useful result in one session | 5 of 8 reach first result within one session without help | Sign-ups from a landing page with no use |
| Retention intent (behavioral) | Ask them to book a second session or bring real data back | 4 of 8 return with their own data within a week | "I'd definitely use it again" |
| Workflow replacement | Ask which current tool or step it would replace, then check it | 3 of 8 name the tool they would drop and the step they would hand over | Page views or demo attendance |

## Website and marketing (sites, campaigns, comms)

Typical riskiest assumption: the right audience cares about this offer enough to act.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fake-door conversion | A page or button for an offer that does not exist yet, clearly labeled "coming soon"; count clicks to the next step | 8% of qualified visitors click "Get early access" on the new offer page | Total traffic or impressions |
| Reply, click, or save rate | Send the message to a defined list; count replies, link clicks, saves, forwards | 12 replies from 60 cold emails that ask a specific question | Open rates alone or likes from friends |
| Waitlist sign-ups | A short sign-up form with a clear consent line (what you will send, how to leave) and nothing collected you do not need | 40 sign-ups from the target audience in two weeks, with consent recorded | Emails collected without consent, or sign-ups from your own network |
| Message comprehension | 5-second test: show the page, ask what it offers and who it is for | 7 of 10 visitors state the offer correctly after 5 seconds | "Looks professional" |

Name where the traffic came from; a KR on a page nobody was sent to proves nothing.

## Physical product and hardware (devices, tools, industrial components)

Typical riskiest assumption: it works in real conditions and is worth its price to the buyer.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task success in a hands-on session | Give a rough prototype or mock-up and a real task; observe without helping | 7 of 10 testers fill, close, and carry the bottle through a test walk without leaks or help | "It feels sturdy" when held for 10 seconds |
| Stated pre-order intent or LOI | Ask for a written, non-binding intent: quantity, price, timing. For B2B, a signed LOI from a named buyer | 3 plant managers sign non-binding LOIs for 500+ units at the target price | A thumbs-up after a demo; any money taken |
| Price-ladder acceptance | Show three prices in turn and record where they hesitate or refuse | 6 of 10 accept the middle price without pushback | Asking "would you pay $X?" once and taking the yes |
| Trial-unit return rate | Lend units for two weeks; count how many are still in use and how many testers ask to keep one | 5 of 8 loaned units are still in daily use at the end of two weeks | Units returned politely with "great job" and no usage data |

## CPG: food, beverage, beauty, home

Typical riskiest assumption: people prefer it to what they buy now, and a retailer will stock it.
For food, beverage, and beauty samples, check allergen and safety basics before anyone tries one.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blind taste or sample score vs a competitor | Blind side-by-side with the product they buy today, strangers not friends; score and a pick | 12 of 20 strangers pick our sample over the leading brand in a blind test | Friends and family scoring your batch 9 out of 10 |
| Repeat-purchase intent | After two weeks with a sample pack, ask whether they finished it and what they would do next | 8 of 15 testers finished the pack and ask where to buy more | "I'd buy this" at the sampling table |
| Retailer-buyer interest | Pitch the concept and a sample to buyers; record next steps they commit to | 3 of 5 independent retail buyers agree to a follow-up meeting with pricing | A buyer saying "send me a deck" with no next step |
| Shelf-standout (3-second test) | Show a mock shelf or photo of your pack among competitors; ask what they noticed and what it is | 14 of 20 shoppers identify the product type within 3 seconds and pick it out | "Nice packaging" without a task |

Real retail sales or shipping product to the public only after the compliance checks for your
category (food safety, labeling, ingredient and claims rules, cosmetics rules where you sell).

## Fashion and apparel

Typical riskiest assumption: it fits and feels right, and people want it enough to wear it.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fit and comfort from wear tests | Lend samples for two weeks; collect fit, comfort, and wash notes with photos | 8 of 12 wear testers wear the piece at least 3 times and rate fit 4 out of 5 or higher | Compliments at a pop-up the first day |
| Pre-sale interest (no charge) | A look-book or drop page with a "notify me" or size-request button, no payment | 60 size requests from the target audience in three weeks | Social followers or likes |
| Return-reason themes | Ask wear testers (or early requesters) why they would send it back; tag the reasons | Fewer than 2 of 12 testers cite a fit problem; the top reason is color, not size | Counting returns without recording reasons |
| Size-curve signals | Track which sizes testers and requesters ask for and which they struggle to find | The size request spread matches the planned curve within one size at the ends | One enthusiastic fan in a single size |

## Services (marketplaces, local services, consulting, B2B services)

Typical riskiest assumption: customers will use a new way to get this done, and the job can be
delivered at a workable cost.

| Signal | How to collect at prototype stage | Example KR | NOT a validation signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concierge completion | Deliver the service by hand to real customers; count jobs finished end to end | 8 of 10 requested jobs are completed and the customer confirms they would rebook | A long list of people who say they are interested |
| Repeat usage | Offer a second job at the same terms; count who takes it | 4 of 8 customers request a second job within 30 days | A positive survey with no second booking |
| Willingness to pay | State the price in the conversation or on the request form; record accept, haggle, refuse (no money taken at this stage) | 6 of 10 accept the stated price or a range within 15 percent | "Sounds reasonable" without a named amount |
| Time per job | Time each step you do by hand; this decides whether the economics can ever work | Average hands-on time per job falls under 45 minutes by the tenth job | Quality scores with no record of the effort |

For two-sided marketplaces, write at least one KR for each side (providers and customers).

## Quick pick by riskiest assumption

| If the riskiest assumption is... | Start with |
|---|---|
| People will not change their habit | Software: retention intent; Services: repeat usage |
| Nobody will notice or respond | Website: reply/click rate; CPG: shelf-standout test |
| It will not work in real use | Hardware: task success; Fashion: wear-test fit and comfort |
| It is not better than the incumbent | CPG: blind taste vs competitor; Hardware: trial-unit return rate |
| It is not worth the price | Price-ladder acceptance; Services: willingness to pay; LOI |
| The channel will not take it | CPG: retailer-buyer interest; Hardware: installer or distributor LOI |

## Choosing and writing KRs

- Choose signals that fit the riskiest assumption, not the easiest number to collect.
- Each KR should name who (the target person, not "people"), how many, and in what window.
- Pair one behavior signal (they did something) with at most one stated-intent signal (they said
  they would). If a KR can be hit by someone being nice, it is not a validation signal.
- If the founder wants revenue as a KR: at prototype stage, rewrite it as stated intent, a
  waitlist, an LOI, or a price-ladder result.
