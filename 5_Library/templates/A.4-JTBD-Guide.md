

**FOUNDER OS  ·  JOBS TO BE DONE**

**Jobs to be done.**

A founder's field guide to the framework that tells you what to build.

 **WHY THIS MATTERS**

**Most founders are solving the wrong problem.**

There's a story from Clayton Christensen about a fast-food chain that wanted to sell more milkshakes. They ran the usual playbook — customer surveys, focus groups, flavor tests. Nothing moved the needle. Then they tried something different: they watched.

Almost half of all milkshakes were purchased early in the morning, alone, by someone about to drive to work. These buyers weren't impulse-buying sugar. They had a long, boring commute ahead. They wanted something they could eat one-handed, that would keep them full until lunch, and that would make the next forty-five minutes feel less like dead time. They weren't buying a milkshake. They were hiring a milkshake to do a job.

The milkshake's real competitors? Bagels. Bananas. A granola bar. A podcast. Doing nothing. Not other milkshakes. Once you know the job, you know the competition and the competition is nothing like you assumed.

***"People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole. They don't even want the hole. They want the shelf on the wall so the room feels finished."***

This is the reframe at the heart of Jobs to Be Done. Customers don't buy products. They hire them to make progress in a specific circumstance and they fire them the moment something does the job better.

The product you're building is not the point. The job is the point. If you don't know the job, you don't know what you're building, who you're competing with, or whether anyone will stick around after they've signed up.

**WHAT IS JOBS TO BE DONE**

**The job is the progress your customer is trying to make.**

A job to be done is the underlying goal a customer is trying to accomplish independent of any solution. It exists whether or not your product exists. It existed before you started building. It will exist if you shut down tomorrow.

Jobs are stable. Products come and go. The job "get from here to there safely and quickly" has outlasted horses, taxis, and the first generation of ride-share apps. If you build to the product, you're building to something that expires. If you build to the job, you're building to something that lasts.

| THE JOB STATEMENT FORMAT When \[specific situation\], I want to \[motivation or underlying goal\], so I can \[outcome — the progress made\]. *The statement must contain no product, feature, app, or interface. If your solution didn't exist, the job statement would still be true. That's how you know it's clean.* |
| :---- |

**Every job has three layers.**

Founders almost always design for the functional dimension and miss the other two. The emotional and social layers are where adoption is won or lost.

| FUNCTIONAL | The practical task the customer is trying to accomplish. The thing they could describe to anyone without embarrassment. 'Stay full until lunch.' 'Know my stock will be on the shelf Monday.' 'Get the parts to the plant by 5pm.' |
| :---- | :---- |
| **EMOTIONAL** | How the customer wants to feel once this is handled. What they're moving toward or away from. Relief, control, confidence, calm. This is often the real reason they switched. Most products are hired for functional reasons and fired for emotional ones. |
| **SOCIAL** | How the customer wants to be seen by a boss, a client, a peer, a partner. 'Look credible.' 'Come across as organized.' 'Not be the person who dropped the ball.' Not every job has a strong social dimension, but the ones that do are usually stickier. |

**The forces that make people switch or not.**

Knowing the job isn't enough. You need to understand why someone would leave their current solution for yours. Four forces are always in play:

| Push of the current situation *The frustration or friction that makes the old way feel broken. Without this, there's no reason to move.* | Pull of the new solution *The appeal of something better. What draws the customer toward you. More features alone is not pull.* |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Anxiety of the new** *The fear that your solution won't work, will cost too much time to learn, or will create new problems.* | **Habit of the present** *The comfort and inertia of what they already do, even if it's imperfect. 'Good enough' is your most common competitor.* |

*The insight: most startups overspend on Pull (more features, better marketing) when the real blocker is unaddressed Anxiety and Habit. You can win by reducing friction, not just by being better.*

**WHY IT MATTERS**

**Five things JTBD changes for you.**

| 01 | You find out who you're actually competing with. The milkshake didn't compete with other milkshakes. It competed with bananas, bagels, and doing nothing. Your competitive set is defined by the job not the category.  |
| :---- | :---- |

| 02 | You stop building features nobody hired you for. Every feature request gets tested against a single question: which job does this serve? If the answer is vague or missing, you've found roadmap bloat before you've built anything.  |
| :---- | :---- |

| 03 | You know what to measure. The 'so I can' in your job statement is your success metric. Not page views, not feature counts. Did the customer make the progress they came for? The job tells you what done looks like. |
| :---- | :---- |

| 04 | Your whole team argues less. Design, production, marketing, and sales argue endlessly about what and how. The job gives everyone a shared why. |
| :---- | :---- |

| 05 | Your strategy becomes durable. Jobs are stable; solutions are not. Building to the job means your strategy outlasts the product. When the technology shifts, you know what you're still trying to accomplish and that clarity is what lets you adapt without losing direction. |
| :---- | :---- |

**EXAMPLES**

**What a clean job looks like. And what a broken one looks like.**

**Three patterns that break job statements and the fix.**

| WRONG  ·  SOLUTION SMUGGLING *"When I'm out on a run, I want a bottle with a built-in sensor and a companion app that logs every sip, so I can track my hydration."* *"A bottle with a built-in sensor" and "a companion app" describe the solution, not the job. Remove them and the job collapses, which means it was never a real job statement.* |
| :---- |
| **CORRECTED** "When I'm several miles into a long run, I want to know I'm drinking enough without breaking stride, so I can finish strong instead of cramping up." |

| WRONG  ·  VAGUE TRIGGER AND VAGUE OUTCOME *"When something needs fixing at home, I want a platform that connects me with service providers, so I can get things done."* *"Needs fixing" has no trigger. "A platform" is a product. "Get things done" means nothing. This job statement could describe almost any services business ever started.* |
| :---- |
| **CORRECTED** "When something breaks at home on a weekday and I'm at work, I want a vetted local tradesperson who can come within a day, so I can stop worrying about it without taking time off." |

| WRONG  ·  WRONG SUBJECT — THIS IS YOUR GOAL, NOT THEIRS *"When customers open the box, I want them to post a photo wearing the jacket, so I can grow our social reach."* *The subject of a job statement is always the customer. "So I can grow our social reach" describes what the founder needs, not the job the customer is trying to get done.* |
| :---- |
| **CORRECTED** "When a new drop arrives, I want to know how to wear it with what I already own, so I can step out confident the first day." |

**Three jobs that are written clean.**

| MILKSHAKE  ·  FUNCTIONAL \+ EMOTIONAL "When I start a long solo commute, I want something filling I can eat one-handed, so I can make the drive feel less monotonous without slowing down." |
| :---- |

| PACKAGED SNACK  ·  EMOTIONAL "When I pack my kid's lunch on a rushed morning, I want a snack I'm confident is okay for them, so I can feel I've done right by them without reading a label." |
| :---- |

| INDUSTRIAL COMPONENT  ·  FUNCTIONAL + SOCIAL "When a seal fails mid-shift, I want a replacement that fits the existing housing and arrives within hours, so I can get the line running again and be seen as the maintenance lead who kept output on track." |
| :---- |

**HOW FOUNDER OS USES IT**

**How Founder OS uses JTBD.**

JTBD is not a standalone exercise. In Founder OS, it is the foundation that everything else sits on. Here is the chain and what each step produces.

| STAGE 01 JTBD The job your customer is trying to get done — written as a clean, solution-agnostic statement. The when, the motivation, the outcome. No product in it. | STAGE 02 PRD The product requirements document. The job statement becomes your problem framing. The three dimensions become your requirements. The 'so I can' becomes your success metric. | STAGE 03 PROTOTYPE A testable hypothesis. You are not asking 'do they like it?' You are watching whether the customer can make the progress the job describes. The job is your acceptance criteria. |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |

**What this means in practice.**

**The job statement is your problem framing.**  The "Background" section of your PRD is not a list of features your competitors have. It is the job, the customer, and the circumstance written precisely enough that any new team member could read it and know what you are trying to accomplish and for whom.

**The three dimensions become requirements.**  Functional → core requirements. Emotional → experience requirements — how it should feel to use, wear, taste, or receive what you build. Social → how it positions the user to others. These get specified, not assumed.

**The outcome is your metric.**  'So I can finish strong instead of cramping up' is measurable. Do customers report finishing runs feeling better? Do they reorder or come back voluntarily? Do they tell someone else? The job tells you what to instrument before you start building.

**The prototype tests the hypothesis.**  You are not validating features. You are validating whether the job gets done with less friction than what the customer was doing before. If it does: move forward. If it does not: learn why, and iterate. The job stays fixed. The solution is what changes.

***"The job is not a phase of the project. It is the spine of the whole product."***

**YOUR TOOLS**

**The tools you will use.**

Three tools support your JTBD work. Use them in order: the `jtbd` skill surfaces the jobs, the `jtbd-prioritizer` skill tells you which one to build for first.

|  TOOL 01  ·  DISCOVERY jtbd exercise skill WHAT IT IS A conversational AI skill that guides you through writing 3–5 clean, solution-agnostic job statements in a single sitting. It auto-reads your upstream files (`2_Outputs/.agents/A.3-Value-Prop/Value-Proposition.md` and `2_Outputs/.agents/A.3-User-Persona/User-Persona.md`) and generates pre-populated starting stubs before the first question — no manual setup. For each job it runs a 3-step loop: write and validate the statement → tag the primary layer (functional / emotional / social) → make the AI-role call, but only if AI is part of your product. The AI-role call has exactly four options: Feature, Product core, Not yet, Irrelevant — plus one sentence of rationale. If your product has no AI component, the answer is Irrelevant for every job and the skill simply records it. Output is saved automatically to `2_Outputs/.agents/A.4-JTBD/JTBD.md`. WHEN TO USE IT Before you commit to a build. After new customer conversations. Any time you feel uncertain about whether your job statement describes a real need or a feature you want to build. HOW TO USE IT \> Ask your agent to run the `jtbd` skill. \> The skill loads your upstream context automatically. \> For each job, complete the 3-step loop: statement → layer → AI-role (only if AI is part of the product). \> Continue until you have 3–5 jobs (minimum 3 for prioritization to be meaningful; you may stop after 3 or continue to 4–5). \> Output is written to `2_Outputs/.agents/A.4-JTBD/JTBD.md` — ready for the prioritizer. |  TOOL 02  ·  PRIORITIZATION (OPTIONAL) JTBD-Prioritizer.csv WHAT IT IS An optional lightweight CSV scorer for a quantitative first-cut using Ulwick's Opportunity Algorithm: **Opportunity = Importance + MAX(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)**. Score each job on importance and current satisfaction on a 1–10 scale; a job that is important but poorly served scores highest. Verdict bands: ≥ 15 Prioritize · 12–14 Strong opportunity · 10–11 Consider · < 10 Deprioritize. Use it as a quick manual pass before the conversational prioritizer — not as the primary path. WHEN TO USE IT When you want a fast, quantitative first cut across your jobs before the full conversational session. The math makes the first cut; the `jtbd-prioritizer` skill makes the final call. HOW TO USE IT \> Open `JTBD-Prioritizer.csv`. \> Fill the three columns for each job: When, I want to, So I can. \> Select the job dimension: Functional, Emotional, or Social. \> Score Importance and Current Satisfaction on a 1–10 scale. \> Compute the Opportunity Score by hand (or in a spreadsheet) and read the verdict band. Use the results to inform, not replace, the `jtbd-prioritizer` skill session. |  TOOL 03  ·  PRIORITIZATION (PRIMARY) jtbd-prioritizer skill WHAT IT IS The primary prioritization tool.
